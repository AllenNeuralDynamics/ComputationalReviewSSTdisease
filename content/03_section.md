(sec-selective-vulnerability)=

# Selective Vulnerability of SST Interneurons in Aging and Neurodegeneration

Three quantities stand in for the cell class delimited in {ref}`sec-sst-identity`, in almost every ageing and disease study:

- the somatostatin peptide
- the *SST* transcript
- a soma scored positive for one of them

These are routinely read as interchangeable indices of one population. They are not, and the distinction organises everything that follows.

The somatostatin signal declines with normal ageing by magnitudes of the same order as those disease cohorts attribute to pathology {cite:p}`Hayashi1997, Florio1991`, though the comparison runs across different species, analytes and comparators {cite:p}`Poirel2018`. It declines again after many acute insults, though not all {cite:p}`Johansen1987, Freund1992`.

But three bodies of evidence cut against reading that decline as fragility of the cells:

- the one study that counts cells independently of the peptide {cite:p}`Komitova2013`
- the marker-based counts that barely move in the same aged cortex {cite:p}`Cha1997`
- the studies that delete the proposed vulnerability mechanisms {cite:p}`Bouilleret2000`

None of them supports an account of SST interneurons as intrinsically fragile. Vulnerability across these studies is better described as region- and insult-specific than as a property of the cell class.

(sec-03-aging-baseline)=

## Age-related decline of the somatostatin signal

Across species and assay families the somatostatin transcript falls steeply in the aged brain. Three ageing series set the magnitude:

- in macaque, the ratio of SRIF mRNA to G3PDH mRNA decreased by 60–70% between 2 years and more than 30 years of age, in hippocampus and in cerebral subdivisions reported as including frontal, temporal, motor, somatosensory and visual cortex {cite:p}`Hayashi1997`
- in 25-month-old rats, pre-prosomatostatin mRNA was reduced by 49% in frontal cortex, 80% in parietal cortex and 69% in striatum, with no change in beta-actin gene expression {cite:p}`Florio1991`
- in Fischer 344 rats, hypothalamic somatostatin gene expression in old animals was approximately 50% of the level in young animals, while cortical expression was similar in young, middle-aged and old rats {cite:p}`SONNTAG1990`

The two rodent series each reproduce the direction of the other but disagree region by region, and the disagreement is not cosmetic: the region that falls in one is the region that does not move in the other.

What does not fall, in the same aged rodent cortex, is the number of cells. Immunohistochemical counts give only slight decreases of somatostatin-immunoreactive cell numbers in parietal and occipital cortex of aged animals {cite:p}`Cha1997`. The hypothalamic decline was attributed by its own authors to decreased expression within specific hypothalamic nuclei, and not to a loss of somatostatin-containing neurons {cite:p}`SONNTAG1990`.

A transcriptional mechanism fits that reading. In human orbital frontal cortex, methylation of the *SST* 5' UTR and first exon/intron correlates negatively with *SST* expression (r = −0.48, p < 0.01 and r = −0.63, p < 0.001) {cite:p}`McKinney2015`. The correlation is compatible with reduced expression in surviving cells, though the measurement is made on bulk tissue and does not localise to them.

The magnitudes matter downstream. The 42.4% reduction of somatostatin expression reported in prefrontal area BA9 is a contrast between advanced dementia (CDR 5) and milder severity within one cohort, and not disease against age-matched control {cite:p}`Poirel2018`. It is also smaller than the 49–80% reductions that ageing alone produces in rodent cortical transcript measures {cite:p}`Florio1991` — a different species, somatostatin peptide measured by radioimmunoassay against a transcript measure, and a different comparator. Age matching is therefore the condition under which a disease effect can be claimed at all, and not a methodological courtesy.

The same ambiguity runs through every bulk readout in human tissue. Reduced cortical somatostatin-like immunoreactivity in Alzheimer tissue was established by radioimmunoassay four decades ago {cite:p}`Davies1980`. Orthogonal assays have since reproduced it: tandem mass tag proteomics put somatostatin 10-fold down in frontal cortex {cite:p}`Sathe2020`, and RNA sequencing of ten matched post-mortem brains gave a log fold-change of −2.2 for *SST* in the precuneus {cite:p}`Guennewig2021`.

In cerebrospinal fluid the deficit is not diagnosis-specific:

- somatostatin was 17.5 ± 5.0 pg/mL in dementia of the Alzheimer type against 23.1 ± 8.2 pg/mL in age-matched controls {cite:p}`Atack1988`
- it was 88.0 ± 4.1 pg/mL in 39 parkinsonian patients against 147.3 ± 5.1 pg/mL in controls {cite:p}`Dupont1982`
- it was reduced again in a separate Parkinson's disease series, with no correlation to dementia score {cite:p}`Strittmatter1996a`

In both of the older cohorts the reduction was unrelated to severity or duration {cite:p}`Atack1988, Dupont1982`, which already argues against reading the peptide as a progression marker; {ref}`sec-human-evidence` assembles that evidence as a series.

Not one of these assays counts a cell; {numref}`fig-sec3-marker-level-readouts-transcript-peptide-csf` sets the three readout families side by side. Where the cellular correlate has been examined directly, the morphological substrate of reduced cortical somatostatin and neuropeptide Y content was reported to be distortion and reduction of fibre plexuses instead of cell disappearance {cite:p}`Kowall1988`. Two further observations are consistent with that reading: somatostatin-immunoreactive processes reside within neuritic plaques {cite:p}`Morrison1985`, and somatostatin and choline acetyltransferase deficits correlate in association areas 9 and 22 {cite:p}`Dournaud1995`.

One limit applies to the older postmortem and cerebrospinal-fluid series as a whole: 26 of 80 findings in this body of work are documented beyond the abstract, so assay details that would adjudicate between these readings are frequently unavailable.

:::{admonition} Evidence Conflict
:class: warning
**Transcript versus cell count in the same aged rodent cortex.** {cite:t}`Florio1991` report pre-prosomatostatin mRNA reductions of 49-80% across aged rat forebrain, while {cite:t}`Cha1997` report only slight decreases in somatostatin-immunoreactive cell numbers in aged rat cortex. The disagreement is one of measurement modality and of what was counted, not of direction: a transcript pool can collapse in neurons that remain in place. Status: resolved by method, in the sense that the two measures are not measuring the same thing - but the resolution removes the cell-loss reading from the ageing literature rather than reconciling it.

(sec-03-hilar-lesion)=

## The hilar lesion model and what it does and does not generalise

The strongest demonstration of somatostatin-cell vulnerability anywhere in this field comes from the dentate hilus after acute insult, and it is genuinely strong. Transient forebrain ischaemia in rat produced a significant 60–80% loss of hilar and CA3c somatostatin neurons, quantitated at day 1, 2, 3 and 4, with damage significant on the second postischemic day and preceding the delayed loss of CA1 neurons {cite:p}`Johansen1987`. Two minutes of ischaemia in gerbil — too brief to kill CA1 pyramidal neurons — caused a selective 40% loss of hilar somatostatin neurons at 1 day and 60% at 7 days {cite:p}`Matsuyama1993`. No loss followed 1 minute of ischaemia, and almost complete loss followed repeated 2-minute episodes.

Seizure models put the same population at the top of the ranking. After status epilepticus the hilar somatostatin-containing cells were the most vulnerable cell type, while parvalbumin- and calbindin-containing nonprincipal neurons were remarkably resistant {cite:p}`Freund1992`, and epileptic mice extend the magnitude: hilar somatostatin interneuron numbers fell to 18% of controls {cite:p}`Hofmann2016`. But that ranking is not reproduced everywhere. In progressive-seizure animals, hilar parvalbumin- AND somatostatin-immunoreactive neurons were almost completely eliminated together, while non-progressive animals retained both unilaterally {cite:p}`Gorter2001`, so in that model the loss is not confined to the somatostatin population.

Traumatic and ischaemic lesions converge on the same subfield:

- hilar cell loss after fluid-percussion injury included somatostatin-immunoreactive neurons, with irreversible hilar injury within 4 hours of impact {cite:p}`Lowenstein1992`
- hilar and CA3 interneuronal damage after four-vessel occlusion extended over a wider dorsoventral range than CA1 damage {cite:p}`Hsu1993`
- spiny calretinin-containing hilar neurons degenerated on the same early schedule {cite:p}`Freund1993`

Unbiased stereology in two acquired-epilepsy models resolves the pattern as subtype- and subfield-specific instead of global: neuropeptide Y-immunoreactive dentate neurons fell to 33–37% of control (p < 0.01) while parvalbumin-immunoreactive neurons in the ipsilateral CA1 were at 80% remaining (p < 0.05) {cite:p}`Huusko2013`. Both values come from the post-status-epilepticus arm of that study, which counted somatostatin neurons as well, and neuropeptide Y is co-expressed with somatostatin in only part of this population, so it is a proxy and not a substitute.

So the hilar finding is replicated, cross-species and cross-insult — a result about a hilar population, and not about SST interneurons in general, as {numref}`fig-sec3-sst-depletion-across-insults` makes plain.

Its generality fails in three directions at once. The first is traumatic injury, where the two remaining injury models disagree with one another. After diffuse mild traumatic brain injury, axotomy within the somatostatin population was rare, representing only 10% of the axotomy occurring in GAD67-positive swellings, and somatostatin-to-pyramidal synaptic efficacy was enhanced instead of degraded {cite:p}`Harris2022`. Controlled cortical impact yields significant ipsilateral SST interneuron loss together with a roughly fourfold reduction of long-range input to hippocampal SST interneurons; the distant regions supplying that input showed no accompanying cell loss {cite:p}`Frankowski2022`. Lateral fluid percussion injury yields heterogeneous hippocampal interneuron changes and no obvious loss of any interneuron subset in motor cortex {cite:p}`Carron2019`. Whether traumatic injury depletes this class consistently is therefore unsettled, and the discrepancy tracks the model and the region sampled.

The second is the striatum, where the class is repeatedly spared by peptide content and by cell count alike:

- somatostatin and neuropeptide Y concentrations were unchanged despite a significant 25% depletion of substance P-like immunoreactivity and GABA after transient forebrain ischaemia in gerbil striatum {cite:p}`Uemura1990`
- the same peptides were unaffected within areas of ischaemic damage in neonatal rat {cite:p}`Ferriero1988`
- somatostatin-immunoreactive and cholinergic neurons were relatively preserved in areas of severe neuronal loss in adult gerbil {cite:p}`Chesselet1990`
- somatostatin interneuron numbers were no different from the contralateral hemisphere at 3 and 14 days after mild focal ischaemia {cite:p}`Katchanov2003`

Sparing is not universal there either: striatal somatostatin/NPY interneurons are highly vulnerable to quinolinic acid while cholinergic interneurons are highly resistant {cite:p}`FigueredoCardenas1998`. The contrast is the point: the answer changes with the insult, and not with the marker.

The third is development, where the marker falls without the cells going missing {cite:p}`Komitova2013`. A multi-hit neonatal injury model gives a persistent 32% reduction in the density of somatostatin-immunoreactive interneurons in prefrontal cortex, confined to upper layers, with no marker-independent count reported {cite:p}`Lacaille2019`. In preterm fetal sheep, somatostatin interneuron numbers fell in caudate, putamen, intragyral white matter and entorhinal cortex while the rest of cortex was virtually unaffected {cite:p}`Ardalan2019`. Reported null results include the aluminium tangle model in rabbit cortex, in which cortical somatostatin and NADPH-diaphorase neurons were unaffected {cite:p}`Kowall1989`.

:::{admonition} Evidence Conflict
:class: warning
**Vulnerable class or resilient class?** {cite:t}`Hofmann2016` report hilar somatostatin interneurons reduced to 18% of control in epileptic mice; {cite:t}`Harris2022` report that after mild traumatic brain injury axotomy is largely confined to parvalbumin cells, with somatostatin axotomy representing only 10% of that in GAD67-positive swellings, and somatostatin output enhanced. A parallel disagreement runs between hilus and striatum under acute insult: hilar somatostatin cells are the most vulnerable type after status epilepticus, a death pattern its authors describe as resembling ischaemic damage {cite:p}`Freund1992`, while striatal somatostatin content is unchanged after transient forebrain ischaemia {cite:p}`Uemura1990`. The axis is model construct and circuit context rather than measurement: the same marker yields opposite conclusions about vulnerability in different regions and insults. Status: resolved by method, in favour of insult- and region-specificity over class-intrinsic fragility. The inventory of these disagreements is assembled in {ref}`sec-conflicts-limitations`.
:::

(sec-03-mechanisms)=

## Candidate cell-intrinsic mechanisms and their evidential status

The mechanistic menu offered for hilar and cortical somatostatin-cell vulnerability is short and, measured against what was actually tested, thinly supported; {numref}`fig-sec3-vulnerability-mechanisms-and-their-evidence` audits it mechanism by mechanism.

The dominant cell-intrinsic account is buffer deficiency. Hilar somatostatin-containing cells and CA3 pyramidal cells contain neither parvalbumin nor calbindin-D28K, an observation made in the same preparations that established their ischaemic vulnerability {cite:p}`Freund1990, Freund1992`. But the available causal test runs the other way. Parvalbumin-deficient, parvalbumin/calbindin-deficient and parvalbumin/calretinin-deficient mice show no difference in hippocampal cytoarchitecture or in the number, distribution or morphology of interneurons {cite:p}`Bouilleret2000`, and parvalbumin content does not protect striatal interneurons from quinolinic acid {cite:p}`FigueredoCardenas1998`.

An excitotoxic-signalling account has one experimental anchor: STEP-dependent suppression of ERK/MAPK survival signalling was identified as what renders hilar somatostatinergic interneurons vulnerable to status epilepticus {cite:p}`Choi2007`. It should be read as a single study, and not as a mechanism consolidated over the nineteen years since.

Seizure-linked metabolic stress has been measured in identified cells. Pimonidazole immunoreactivity, probing hypoxia, was present in neuropeptide Y- and somatostatin-labelled neurons, and counts of pimonidazole-positive neurons rose with status epilepticus duration (P < 0.01, 120 versus 60 and 30 minutes) {cite:p}`Gualtieri2012`.

Transcriptional vulnerability to a neuroendocrine stressor has likewise been measured at cell-type resolution in one study, with very few gene changes in pyramidal neurons compared with somatostatin neurons after corticosterone exposure {cite:p}`Lin2015`. But the animals were young adults under chronic stress, not aged or degenerating tissue, so extension of the result to ageing is extrapolation.

:::{admonition} Evidence Conflict
:class: warning
**Does missing calcium buffering explain hilar somatostatin-cell vulnerability?** {cite:t}`Freund1990` report that hilar somatostatin cells contain neither parvalbumin nor calbindin and degenerate earliest, but conclude against any consistent relationship between calcium-binding-protein content and ischaemic vulnerability, since CA3 pyramidal cells are equally buffer-free and resistant; {cite:t}`Bouilleret2000` find that genetically deleting those buffers, singly and in combination, leaves interneuron number, distribution and morphology unchanged. The first is correlative and the second is a direct test, so the inference from marker absence to fragility is not supported by the retrieved evidence, while remaining the most frequently repeated explanation. Status: open - deletion of the buffers is not the same experiment as deletion of the buffers plus an insult, and that experiment was not retrieved by these searches.
:::

What is missing is more striking than what is present. Three of the proposed mechanisms lack the measurement that would test them:

- metabolic load — the explanation most often repeated as the reason this class is fragile — rests on inference from bulk and transcriptomic association, with no per-cell-type measurement of oxygen consumption, ATP demand or reactive-oxygen load in identified somatostatin interneurons to anchor it; {ref}`sec-conflicts-limitations` carries it forward as an open question
- the buffer account rests on marker expression alone, unaccompanied by any measurement of calcium transients or buffering capacity in identified somatostatin cells under degenerative conditions
- the ER-stress account is extrapolated from chronic stress in young adults, with no measurement of the unfolded protein response or of oxidative load in identified somatostatin interneurons of aged animals

BDNF/TrkB signalling sits in the same position. Direct loss-of-function evidence that its reduction causes somatostatin interneuron loss in the aged brain is lacking. What stands in its place is co-decline: somatostatin and BDNF transcripts fall together in aged macaque brain {cite:p}`Hayashi1997`, and their promoters are hypermethylated in parallel in aged human cortex {cite:p}`McKinney2015`.

Receptor-level ageing changes on the cells themselves are better measured:

- somatic GluN2B puncta density on hippocampal O-LM interneurons declines between 3 and 16 months, with GluN1 unchanged {cite:p}`Gramuntell2021`
- a preprint reports reduced GluN1 puncta density and size on cortical Martinotti cells in older animals, most pronounced in females {cite:p}`SanchezMoreno2025`
- mGlu1 activation depolarizes all prelimbic somatostatin interneurons tested in young adult cortex, with no ageing comparison reported {cite:p}`Maksymetz2021`

Genotype and sex modulate the ageing trajectory: female apoE4 knock-in mice lose hilar somatostatin-positive interneurons faster than apoE3 controls (genotype F(1,54) = 23.85, p < 0.005) {cite:p}`Leung2012`. A receptor-side mechanism is proposed by the loss of SSTR2 from human locus coeruleus from Braak stages III/IV onward, with *Sstr2* deletion alone causing progressive noradrenergic degeneration {cite:p}`Adori2015` — a proposal that stands unreplicated eleven years on.

## Cross-disease magnitude, and why the convergence is partly artefactual

Assembled across models, reported depletion runs from roughly a fifth of controls remaining to no change at all. The position a study occupies in that range tracks its denominator and its labelling strategy more closely than its disease.

At the severe end, hilar somatostatin interneurons fell to 18% of controls in epileptic mice {cite:p}`Hofmann2016`, and stereological counting in PS1xAPP hippocampus gave a 50–60% diminution of somatostatin-immunopositive neurons preceding pyramidal cell loss {cite:p}`Ramos2006`.

In the middle sit genetic-injury models that count a reporter-defined subset instead of the whole population. Granule-cell *Pten* deletion reduced eGFP-SST cell density in hilus (4,395 ± 182 to 3,411 ± 322 cells/mm3, p = 0.0334) and CA1 but not CA3 {cite:p}`Drake2024`. Targeted ablation reduced SOM-positive cells in dentate gyrus and CA3 while sparing CA1 {cite:p}`Nagarajan2023`; there the depletion is the manipulation, and not a measured consequence of pathology.

In human tissue the same spread appears across techniques, and it brackets zero. Stereological counting gives a 50% reduction in the number of SST cells in Alzheimer hippocampus (p = 0.0066) {cite:p}`GonzalezRodriguez2021`, while immunohistochemical counts in cortex show no difference in the number of somatostatin neurons between aged controls and cases {cite:p}`Li1996b`. The disagreement tracks region and counting design, not disease severity.

Within cortex, three findings subdivide the population further:

- there are fewer somatostatin interneurons per mm2, but not parvalbumin interneurons, in Alzheimer temporal cortex {cite:p}`Waller2020`
- *SST* mRNA is reduced across layers 2 to superficial 6 of schizophrenia dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, sparing layer 1, deep layer 6 and white matter {cite:p}`Morris2008`
- a subset of upper-layer *Sst* interneurons is depleted in schizophrenia cortex, reported to overlap the subtypes lost earliest in Alzheimer's disease {cite:p}`Endresz2026` — a single recent study whose cross-disease claim is so far unreplicated

No single study quantifies somatostatin interneuron abundance across Alzheimer, Parkinson, Huntington and schizophrenia tissue with one method and one cell-type definition, so the cross-disease range is assembled from assays that are not interchangeable.

Two further considerations block reading this spread as one gradient. The denominator differs: somatostatin neurons constitute only 43–53% of hilar GAD65-expressing neurons, even though 96% of hilar pre-prosomatostatin neurons in the dorsal and 92% in the ventral dentate gyrus are GAD65-positive {cite:p}`Esclapez1995`. So a loss expressed against the inhibitory population and one expressed against the somatostatin population are different numbers.

And the direction itself is not fixed. Somatostatin concentrations are elevated, not reduced, in Huntington disease basal ganglia (caudate 247 ± 24 versus 85 ± 11 ng/gm) {cite:p}`Aronin1983`, while somatostatin density is significantly decreased in the basolateral and central complex of the Parkinson disease amygdala {cite:p}`FloresCuadrado2017`. The comparison sets peptide content measured by assay against cell density measured by counting, so the cross-disease reversal may be partly a reversal of method. Both contrasts are developed in {ref}`sec-huntingtons` and, for the Lewy body cohorts, in {ref}`sec-parkinsons-lewy-body`.

:::{admonition} Evidence Conflict
:class: warning
**Is somatostatin-immunoreactive neuron density actually reduced in Alzheimer-type cortex?** {cite:t}`Waller2020` report fewer somatostatin interneurons per mm2 in Alzheimer temporal cortex, with parvalbumin interneurons unaffected. {cite:t}`Nakamura1986` report that the density of somatostatin-immunoreactive cells in the neocortex did not decrease in senile dementia of the Alzheimer type compared with aged normal subjects, locating the change in fibre terminals instead; that reading is corroborated by the finding that the morphological correlate of reduced cortical somatostatin content is distortion and reduction of fibre plexuses {cite:p}`Kowall1988`. The axis is measurement modality and what was counted - somata per unit area against plexus integrity - compounded by cohort composition. Status: open; the human counting evidence is weighed in {ref}`sec-human-evidence`.
:::

(sec-03-testable)=

## What would have to be true for the class-level claim to hold

The class-level vulnerability claim requires one experiment: counting cells by an identity independent of somatostatin, in the same tissue in which the somatostatin signal falls. That experiment exists in rodents, and it does not support the strong reading. Hypoxic-reared mice carrying a GAD67-EGFP reporter showed no change in total labelled interneuron number and no evidence of increased interneuron death, so the fall in marker-detectable somatostatin cells was a change in marker expression {cite:p}`Komitova2013`. The same logic applied to hypothalamic transcript decline in ageing attributed it to reduced expression within nuclei instead of neuron loss {cite:p}`SONNTAG1990`.

Genetic labelling narrows the effect wherever it is used. In a disease model the measured changes are smaller and more layer-specific than marker counts suggest {cite:p}`Drake2024`, and marker counts in other models are layer-restricted too, with the proportion of SST-immunoreactive interneurons reduced in stratum oriens but not stratum pyramidale {cite:p}`Marks2016`. The equivalent human experiment — lineage-independent labelling paired with peptide measurement in the same tissue — is not available, which is why the human claim remains inferential; {ref}`sec-human-evidence` takes up that gap.

Two further constraints sharpen what is at stake. Loss need not be complete to matter functionally, but it must be substantial: translamellar inhibition in the dentate survived less than 40% hilar neuron loss and was consistently abolished only beyond 85% {cite:p}`Zappone2004`. And the cells that survive remodel {cite:p}`Zhang2009`:

- they hypertrophy
- they extend dendrites
- they sprout new synapses onto granule cells

Conversely, silencing is sufficient without death. Chemogenetic inhibition of hilar *Sst* interneurons produced a 24.6% increase in dentate c-Fos staining (P < 0.001) {cite:p}`Lyu2023`, and partial ablation produced dentate hyperactivity {cite:p}`Nagarajan2023`, so the circuit consequence that disease studies attribute to cell loss is obtainable from loss of output alone. An amyloid overexpression model makes the point from the other side, reporting decreased cholinergic drive onto O-LM interneurons as its pathomechanism without counting a missing cell {cite:p}`Schmid2016`. A knock-in model at endogenous dosage detects no significant change in SOM interneuron density in CA1 stratum oriens even at 24 months, in a comparison of three wild-type mice (p = 0.6625) {cite:p}`Sos2020`. Dysfunction and depletion are therefore separable — but whether the difference between these two reports comes from construct dosage or from what each study counted is unresolved on the present evidence.

The ageing and lesion literatures establish the measurement problem in general terms. {ref}`sec-alzheimers` is where it carries the highest stakes, because the Alzheimer somatostatin deficit is at once the most reproducible observation in the field and the basis of a proposed causal mechanism.
